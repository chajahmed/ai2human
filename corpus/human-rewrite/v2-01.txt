It's important to understand the rationale behind these two entities by going back a bit in time. Think back to the late 1960s. The car business in America was on the upswing, but the factory was in big trouble. Whenever a new model of car was introduced by General Motors, they would have to remove tons of wire and relays and redo the wiring again. It costs a lot of money, took a long time, and made everyone so mad. So they concluded, "There must be a better way. Plug in a brainy, tough little box: the PLC. It was designed to weather the storm of a factory: the heat, the grease, the vibrations and the electricity that would cook a standard computer. It was a survivor. Let's go back to the 1980s. The costs of energy were spiraling upwards and buildings were becoming more sophisticated.The price of energy was rising, and buildings were becoming more complex. The hissing, air-operated thermostats didn't work anymore. Building owners had to find something better to control their heating, cooling and ventilation. Enter the DDC. It wasn't created on a filthy factory floor; it was created in a clean, controlled, mechanical room, built for comfort and efficiency, not speed, and muscle. From the outset, these two were very different in their upbringing.
