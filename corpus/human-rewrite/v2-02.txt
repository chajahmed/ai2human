So let's move onto the hardware, or the physical aspects. There's a clear difference between holding a PLC in one hand and a DDC in the other. The PLC is a tank. Constructed with military-grade components, including heavy-duty terminals, circuit boards protected with a special coating to repel moisture and dust, and a thick metal shell. It's supposed to continue for decades, and for years round the clock, in a location with extreme temperature changes and air filled with oil mist or concrete dust. It does not whine, it just goes on. In the inside, it has a speed-focused strong processor. It's just a matter of milliseconds. Microseconds, even. It reads through its programme, looks at its inputs, updates its outputs and repeats this over and over, each step faster than you can blink. In contrast, a DDC controller is… well, it's a lot more modest. It sort of resembles an expensive thermostat or a bulky network switch. It's smaller, lighter and can be mounted on a wall in a clean mechanical room or even inside an air handling unit. It's not for war – it's for comfort. It has the power to perform its functions – to control temperatures and pressures – but is not going to win any speed contests. The good news is, a DDC system employs a team of these little controllers, rather than a single large and powerful brain like a PLC system. These each take care of a small segment of the construction. Consider a sports team with a position for everyone, versus one player with the quarterback role trying to do everything himself.
