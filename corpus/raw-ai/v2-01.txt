To really get why these two exist, we have to go back in time a little bit. Picture the late 1960s. America's car industry was booming, but there was a huge headache on the factory floor. Every time General Motors wanted to build a new car model, they had to tear out massive panels of wiring and relays and rewire everything from scratch. It was expensive, it took forever, and it drove everybody crazy. So they said, "There has to be a better way." Enter the PLC—a rugged, brainy little box that could be reprogrammed instead of rewired. It was built to survive the chaos of a factory: the heat, the grease, the vibration, the electrical noise that would fry a regular computer. It was a survivor. Now, fast forward to the 1980s. Energy prices were going through the roof, and buildings were getting more complex. The old pneumatic systems—those hissing, air-powered thermostats—just weren't cutting it anymore. Building owners needed something smarter to manage their heating, cooling, and ventilation. Enter the DDC. It wasn't born on a dirty factory floor; it was born in a clean, climate-controlled mechanical room, with a focus on comfort and efficiency rather than speed and muscle. Right from the start, these two had completely different upbringings.
