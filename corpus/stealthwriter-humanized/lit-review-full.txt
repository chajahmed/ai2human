Abstract
The increasing uptake of EV's in all countries across the world is creating a new and growing fire safety threat in urban built environments – the problem is lithium-ion battery thermal runaway, an exothermic chain reaction that can reach temperatures exceeding 700°C and is self-propelled. Conventionally, fire suppression systems do not have the means to effectively prevent it. This presents special risks in multi-story charging garages, underground car parks and EV bays within commercial and residential buildings. However, there is currently no fire risk framework that appears to be developed to respond to this dynamism. Electrochemical monitoring of batteries, urban spatial fire risk and safety of EV charging stations. Three streams of research, uncombined and not integrated into a working framework. AI thermal runaway prediction, computer vision fire detection, GIS urban risk mapping and machine learning, probabilistic Bayesian methods, digital twins, IoT safety management, EV charging checks. However, this is a real and uncompromised difference. There is no prior literature that links the ML-based thermal runaway prediction to the spatial translation to continuously updated building zone-level fire risk maps. The current frameworks cannot adequately manage the fire risk of EVs in buildings. The goal of this study is to combine a hybrid LSTM-CNN model using real-time battery sensor information to predict the onset of thermal runaway and then transfer these predictions to the whole building through a zone-level classification of the risk. Building managers and responding fire officers are provided with a live dashboard view of the integrated picture of the risk as it evolves. Validation with static probabilistic baselines and expert assessed fire scenarios. What is not available today, and the concept is edge-deployable and dynamic system for EV fire risk analysis.


1. Introduction

New fire patterns are emerging with the global transition to EVs that are not accounted for in safety codes. Thermal runaway: A self-reinforcing exothermic reaction, all major EV platforms use lithium-ion batteries, can get above 700 °C and continue from cell to cell before normal alarms are activated. This pre-ignition window is the reason that real-time computer-vision systems like the multi-criteria risk-scored detector that was deployed at EV charging stations by Gao et al. (2023) are designed for. EV battery fires can also re-flash, sometimes hours after the fire is believed to be out and in restricted areas that would be difficult to extinguish, underground car parks, multi-story buildings and high-density urban charging centers where ventilation is poor. A similar profile is described by Elcin Eroglu (2026) for an underground battery-electric mining fleet, where there is a limited means of escape and poor air flow which increases the likelihood of ignition and the difficulty of suppression. Confined space does not only apply to urban car parks and can be found with battery-electric vehicles in any enclosed area.

The fire hazard in EV environments is constantly evolving as the battery SOC changes, degradation history, ambient temperature, mechanical stress, and charging rate vary (Ye et al., 2025). Data-driven early warning methods have significantly enhanced detection capabilities: multi-signal fusion approaches, which combine voltage, temperature, gas, and mechanical signals, deliver detection delays of much more than what is provided by single-sensor approaches (Ye et al., 2025). The literature has not yielded an integration layer. Any published framework doesn't account for signals at the battery level and map those into a spatial map at the building level that could be useful to emergency responders or urban planners. Building scale models indicate finer spatial resolution is possible. Wei et al. (2026) uses the gradient-boosting technique to assign individual buildings to scores based on inspection records, occupancy type and neighborhood density, rather than on city-wide zoning. The cost is temporal: the scores are updated only when administrative records change, not from the live sensor streams.
To provide a snapshot of the landscape of AI and ML techniques that could be relevant to a dynamic EV fire risk tool, this literature review synthesizes 74 peer-reviewed papers from 2023 to 2026. The studies were found by using the Scopus search engine, which was used to search for 2016–2021 publications combining the search terms: thermal runaway prediction, EV battery fire, urban fire risk assessment, digital twin fire safety, Bayesian network fire risk, and computer vision smoke detection. Conference papers, more peripheral work, and papers that were not directly applicable to AI-based fire risk modelling, were not retained. The six thematic clusters together cover technical aspects that would be required for a real-time, spatially aware EV fire risk system.

2. AI and Machine Learning for EV Battery Thermal Runaway Detection and Prediction
Lithium-ion batteries used in EVs are most likely to fail by thermal runaway. The detection methods have evolved from merely threshold monitoring to multi-signal fusion architectures, which enable warning lead times of less than a second (Ye et al., 2025), but reliable warning for a wide range of battery chemistries and platforms remains unsolved for real-time applications. This section contains 17 studies, which are grouped into four technical areas: Data-Driven State Estimation and Fault Diagnosis; Multi-Signal Fusion for Early Warning; Recurrent and Temporal Deep Learning for Sequence Prediction; and Physics-Informed, Graph Neural Network, and Interpretable Approaches.

2.1 Data-Driven State Estimation and Fault Diagnosis
The early data-driven solutions to battery safety largely looked at it as a way to capture faults within the battery management system. Sun (2023) has suggested a hybrid design combining CNN with LSTM. They pre-rank the input signals using Pearson correlation prior to training. This feature selected heterogeneous setup outperforms single type architectures in fault classification for multiple classes particularly with small training samples. Instead, Zhang et al. (2022) took on the state-of-health. Their model is fairly accurate in estimating capacity and detects potential capacity loss in advance which typically causes thermal issues. The merging of fault detection and health estimation into one diagnostic pipeline is slowly beginning to happen. These are not so much different jobs now.
S and Eswari (2025) introduced a hybrid system that utilizes CNNs along with gradient boosting to continuously monitor and detect anomalies in EV battery safety systems. By proactively intervening in the battery state, continuous state inference also significantly lowered overheating incidents, validating the use of predictive analytics within battery management loops. Machlev et al. (2024) reviewed deep learning techniques for fault diagnosis and prognosis of EVs, and they found that ConvLSTM and feed-forward networks were the most commonly used architectures, as well as three recurrent challenges: (1) rare availability of labeled fault data, (2) the need for explainability in safety-critical applications, and (3) on-board computational constraints. Shuhui et al. (2025) followed that up by using a CNN to predict the underlying cause of thermal runaway events onboard, separating out abuse mechanisms from those due to aging and internal short circuits. However, in a critical architectural review, Le et al. (2026) demonstrated on the one hand that transformer-based models outperform LSTMs in the tracking of the long-term temporal dependencies that underlie the estimation of the state of health, while on the other hand, generalization across chemistries and manufacturers has yet to be addressed.


2.2 Multi-Signal Fusion for Early Warning

Single-signal monitoring is less effective when introducing early warning systems: either shorter warning time, or higher misses or both (Ye et al., 2025). In the literature of fusion, the use of different modalities for the fusion of complementary signal types is always superior to using just one modality. For fast-charging sessions, Li et al. (2024) showed that a BiLSTM-Transformer model was able to better estimate the SoH during fast-charging sessions than during variable driving loads. Standardized charging cycles prove to be a helpful period of observation in this respect. Gajghate et al. (2025) moved a step further with introducing a Transformer-based multimodal approach that incorporates time series of impedance, voltage, current and temperature, using cross-attention modules. It performed better than unimodal LSTM and CNN baselines and could be able to identify the thermal runaway risk from specific abuse mechanisms instead of just identifying higher risk.

Ye et al. (2025) discussed signal characteristics, the performance of the fusion algorithm, and if systems are ready for deployment. The signal types were voltage, temperature, gas and mechanical signals. The answer they've discovered is very simple. The most popular options are voltage and temperature, as they are inexpensive and becoming abundant. In contrast, gas sensors are more complex to install, require environmental compensation, and are slower to install. But the problem is. The risk of thermal runaway is not consistently indicated by any one signal type for all types of battery chemistry. So, adaptive fusion strategies are needed. Zhou (2026) attempted something similar but for Battery Electric Ships. They used FDEMATEL-ISM structural analysis, in conjunction with a Bayesian network, for forward probability reasoning over fire and blackout risk states based on different electrical faults. It demonstrated that multi-source causal reasoning helps to achieve a better accuracy for continuous risk monitoring than single-signals threshold-based approaches. Multi-sensor fusion alone isn't enough.

YOLO family (You Only Look Once) is currently the most widely used method of fire and smoke detection in the real world. Uses a one-pass detection pipeline (one stage inference). Which makes it quick, and has good throughput when it comes to actual use. Talaat and ZainEldin (2023) enhanced the YOLOv8 model with attention mechanisms for fire and smoke detection in urban surveillance. It reached the state of the art in the field of smart city application. This is the opposite approach taken by Sun et al. (2023). They developed a lightweight CNN, not a maximization accuracy model. The embedded cameras are also resource-constrained, but real-time fire and smoke detection still exists. Those are too lightweight for the larger attention-enhanced YOLOv8 models. Hasan et al. (2024) instead attempted to concentrate on interpretability. They enhanced a modified YOLOv8 with Grad-CAM attention visualization. That produces spatial evidence maps. Emergency teams can then confirm and verify the detection results. Which things are important in life-safety applications. The lightweight model of Sun et al. (2023) does not include that option.

YOLOGX has been developed by Li et al. (2025) for the purpose of monitoring fires from the air. They installed a cross-attention module into the YOLO structure. The concept was to detect long-range spatial dependencies of the visual features. It outperforms the older models on fire and smoke benchmark data sets. That's because urban surveillance by drones has a scale dependency and resolution issue depending on the altitude. In particular, in EV rich districts.
